MURDER IS PERMISSIBLE FOR TWO REASONS

In the geopolitical space and in the rights granted in the United States, self-defense is a legal shield that allows one to kill other humans to preserve a person’s or nation's existential right to exist. But there are gray areas.

Take the instance of two countries that border one another: Country A begins to amass an invasion-sized force at the border. Country B has two options: Option A is to wait until the invasion happens and amass a defense on their border, and Option B is to attack Country A to get the upper hand. Both of these are valid and legal reasons for war. Option A is called self-defense and is reactive. Option B, however, carries less validity, but is legal, and is designated as a preemptive strike for anticipatory self-defense.

The first justification for murder is on the soldier’s burden. Soldiers have the legal backing of the State to murder for State-backed reasons. The moral and ethical ramifications are internal to the soldier. On the side of the aggressor, the moral and ethical weight is one that they have transgressed. On the side of the defense, regardless of action, they have the moral and ethical right. This is not meaningless because soldiers take this into account. 

When we come to civil law, there is only Option A, where it has to be in self-defense, and it is based around the concept of imminence. Imminence means the threatened harm is about to happen right now. It's immediate, impending, on the verge of occurring, as opposed to something that already happened or that might happen at some point down the road. You may use force when you reasonably believe it's necessary to protect yourself against an imminent unlawful attack. This is the second type of justified murder.

When we look at civil wars and sectarian violence, we are presented with an interesting dilemma regarding legality and legitimacy. When a rebel force initiates violence, it always violates the State law. However, the rebel force may be morally and ethically legitimate; they are right in their actions.

For a rebel force to be protected under international law, a list of grievances must be publicly vocalized and written that violates the group(s)' right of self-determination. Once internal conflict kicks off, the UN can call a Recognition of Belligerency, which elevates the rebel force from an internal group to one of international status if the rebel force: has a general and a standing army, holds occupied land, and has rallied around the stated grievances. If those don’t happen, the rebels are viewed as domestic terrorists internationally, and will always be viewed as domestic terrorists by the State.
