ON PERENNIALISM

The philosophy of the Civilizational Man is an offshoot of Traditionalism, Aldous Huxley’s Perennialism, and Julius Evola’s work. Religion is an aspect of this philosophy, but it is secondary to the primary focus, which is certain historical and empirical truths about humanity that have given rise to modernity.

In the same vein that Arthur Schopenhauer uses the term "Will" as a concept to project his ideas, I too am using the term "perennialism." When I talk about perennialism, I invoke the rules, laws, and social cohesion that allowed the human animal to exist for 300,000+ years. Not just religious perennialism, but an underlying reality of human existence. That allowed it to build and thrive for at least three interglacial periods. That allowed it to live in groups along the coasts, now submerged, in milder climates.

Much like Perennialism attempts to define a lineage of metaphysical truths underpinning religious belief, I attempt to define the lineage of human values and beliefs that produce a lasting civilization. I define a society as a like-minded group of people that share institutions, norms, culture, and a border. I define a civilization as a series of societies that share a cultural influence.

My perennial premises are as follows:
    1. Human existence is hundreds of thousands of years old.
    2. Most of that existence has been environmentally non-conducive to industrial civilization, being too warm, too cold, or reeling from geological or cosmic disruption. The Holocene stability of the last ~12,000 years is the exception, not the norm.
        a) Civilizational knowledge is not self-sustaining. Advanced techniques persist only as long as the specialists who practice them and the infrastructure that supports them remain intact.
        b) When those specialists and their supply chains disperse, the knowledge is effectively lost, because documentation preserves description but not the tacit expertise and infrastructure required to act on it.
    3. This loss is graduated: infrastructure-dependent techniques (electronics, industrial power) vanish first, while distributed, elementary knowledge (agriculture, husbandry) is far more durable.
    4. Our current civilization arose from, and depends on, this anomalous stability.
    5. That stability is not guaranteed to persist. If the environmental baseline of premise 2 reasserts itself, through climatic, geological, or cosmic disruption, industrial civilization cannot be sustained, and humanity would revert toward a pre-industrial existence.

Based on this, there is a common set of values, ethics, and understanding that allows the human species to survive in the darkest times. The philosophy outlined in this book attempts to identify those elements through realizations and a proposed set of laws.
